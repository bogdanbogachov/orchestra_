3. **Headline gain and significance tests.** We will clarify that the **+2.6-2.8 F1** headline is vs default head C0, not the fairer custom last-token C3. 4. **Energy/carbon framing.** We will reframe energy savings as convergence-speed, not lower per-step cost. The heads have similar per-step cost; differences mainly come from early stopping. Attention vs mean uses fewer steps/energy on BANKING77 noisy (**1823 ± 124** vs **2505 ± 117**, **0.27 ± 0.02** vs **0.38 ± 0.02 kWh**) and CLINC150 noisy (**1973 ± 129** vs **3025 ± 296**, **0.36 ± 0.02** vs **0.55 ± 0.06 kWh**). 5. **Noise protocol.** The noise experiment measures robustness to representative randomized production-like noise, not arbitrary unseen shift. We will state that code-switching, ASR, and multi-intent turns are future stress tests, not central current workload.

7. **FFT protocol.** The same noisy BANKING77 split was used for the FFT cutoff sweep and final reporting, so we will treat FFT as exploratory and add this limitation. Since submission, we also ran preliminary adaptive-cutoff checks; they did not change the conclusion, but we will treat them as exploratory unless space permits a full controlled report. Future work needs a separate tuning split or nested validation.

8. **CLINC150 OOS and clean/noisy split.** CLINC OOS examples were included as a regular **151st label** in both training and evaluation. For noise, exact noisy strings are not reused, but broad noise mechanisms are shared. A clean-train/noisy-test condition would better measure distribution shift; we will add it if time permits or list it as future work.

9. **FFT-mean outlier and abstract.** We will add a sentence on clean BANKING77 FFT-mean: **82.17 ± 2.30 F1** vs **88.65 ± 0.54** for non-FFT mean, suggesting fixed spectral truncation can remove useful information when no noise is present. ## Reviewer Comment 2

Thank you for your comments.
1. **Backbone generality.** We agree this is a limitation and will make it more explicit. Our target is cost-sensitive industrial deployment with small language models, hence the focus on Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct rather than larger backbones. This was a deliberate design choice to reduce inference/deployment cost. Prior work suggests pooling behavior can transfer across transformer variants (Ennadir et al., 2025), but we do not claim universal accuracy gains from one backbone. To partially address this, we added DistilBERT with and without attention pooling; the added results show that the training-energy benefit of attention transfers across backbones, while accuracy gains remain dataset-dependent.

2. **Industrial/end-to-end evaluation.** We added the real-log evaluation described above and focus it on the deployed bottleneck: query/SQL-template selection. This is the component replaced by our method; downstream database retrieval and final LLM summarization are shared by both systems. On the anonymized five-intent real subset, the incumbent Titan+Claude selector proxy reaches **81.05%**, while our best classifier reaches **92.64 ± 1.67 acc / 92.57 ± 1.71 F1**.

3. **Proxy datasets vs procurement queries.** BANKING77 and CLINC150 match the task structure, not the domain vocabulary: short user utterance -> one intent. This is also the observed procurement pattern: in our real subset, the median query length is **6 words**, **473/607** queries are <=8 words, and many are direct operational requests with IDs or entities. They do not fully represent manufacturing vocabulary, which is why we added the real procurement evaluation.

4. **Intent/noise representativeness.** The real logs support the synthetic noise design. Observed user behavior includes short typed questions, incomplete references, IDs/part/order numbers, casing/punctuation variation, typos, abbreviations, and supplier/part wording. The dataset is small enough to audit manually, and the six noise mechanisms were chosen to reflect these observations. We will clarify that this is representative of observed typed procurement usage, not ASR/code-switching or arbitrary distribution shift.

Reference: Ennadir et al. (2025), *Pool Me Wisely: On the Effect of Pooling in Transformer-Based Models*, NeurIPS. https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.03339

## Reviewer Comment 3

Thank you for your comments.
1. **Backbone transferability.** We will revise the claim: our original empirical evidence is for one decoder-only SLM, Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct. Broader relevance was motivated by prior pooling work (Ennadir et al., 2025), not proven by the original grid. To partially address this, we added DistilBERT with and without attention pooling; these runs show that attention's training-energy benefit transfers across backbones, while accuracy gains remain dataset-dependent.

2. **FFT details.** We will specify that inputs are tokenizer-padded to `max_length`; no additional zero-padding or length-normalized FFT is used. The base model and final pooling use the attention mask, but FFT filtering is applied before pooling over the full hidden-state sequence with a fixed 40% cutoff and no windowing or learnable spectral filter. Padding, length variation, and boundary artefacts may therefore confound the FFT result. We will frame FFT as exploratory; preliminary post-submission adaptive-cutoff checks did not change the conclusion, but we will not make them a main claim without a full controlled report.

3. **Noise generation.** We will add an appendix table/figure. Each query receives one noise fragment with probability **0.70** or two with **0.30**; fragment type is sampled uniformly from six categories. Self-correction is attempted with probability **0.18 train / 0.12 test**, typos affect only injected fragments with probability **0.20 train / 0.10 test**, and length growth is capped at **45%**. Added-token means are BANKING77 train/test **+3.82/+4.23** and CLINC150 **+2.56/+3.19**. Train/test use separate style pools, so this is a mild lexical/style shift within shared noise families. The same noisy files are used for all methods, so the generator does not selectively favor attention within the controlled comparison.

4. **Lightweight baselines.** We added SBERT-linear and DistilBERT baselines. On the real five-intent subset, SBERT-linear reaches **95.84 F1**, DistilBERT-CLS **94.67 ± 0.81**, DistilBERT-attention **95.49 ± 1.35**, and the best Llama configuration **92.57 ± 1.71**. DistilBERT-attention uses less training energy than CLS on every matched dataset: real **0.0003 vs 0.0009 kWh**, BANKING77 clean/noisy **0.0043/0.0042 vs 0.0047/0.0047**, CLINC150 clean/noisy **0.0064/0.0063 vs 0.0067/0.0068**. Accuracy improves on real/BANKING77 but drops on CLINC150, so we will state that attention can improve training-energy efficiency across backbones, while accuracy is dataset-dependent.

5. **CLINC150 OOS.** OOS examples were included as a regular **151st class**; reported F1 is closed-set multiclass F1 including OOS. We will add one-vs-rest OOS AUROC, FPR@95%TPR using OOS-class probability, and OOS precision/recall/F1. Calibrated rejection thresholds remain future work.

6. **Statistical tests.** We added paired tests over 10 matched seeds. Attention vs custom last-token gives **+0.34 F1** on noisy BANKING77 (`p=0.027`, Holm `p=0.108`, dz `0.83`) and **+0.46 F1** on noisy CLINC150 (`p=0.156`, Holm `p=0.312`, dz `0.49`), so proxy pooling-only gains are not significant after correction. On the real subset, attention beats last-token by **+8.82 F1** (`p=0.000103`, Holm `p=0.000824`, dz `2.08`). We will report p-values, CIs, and effect sizes.

7. **Energy and early stopping.** We will report optimization steps and wall-clock. Energy differences are mainly convergence-speed effects, not lower per-step pooling cost: noisy BANKING77 attention stops at **1823 ± 124 steps / 1492 ± 95 s** vs mean **2505 ± 117 / 2104 ± 94 s**; noisy CLINC150 **1973 ± 129 / 1947 ± 127 s** vs **3025 ± 296 / 2983 ± 297 s**. We will also add inference energy/throughput: real-set Llama attention **15.50 ± 0.44 ms/query**, **0.0006 kWh** test-set inference; DistilBERT-attention **3.47 ± 0.02 ms/query**, energy below table precision. Training energy remains central because the selector may be retrained frequently, potentially weekly.

8. **Attention-weight analysis.** We added a direct analysis of the learned attention-pooling weights on 200 noisy BANKING77 examples. For 184 comparable cases where the original clean query span was recovered, **88.54%** of pooling mass falls on original query tokens vs **11.41%** on injected noise; **81.09%** of top-5 weighted tokens also come from the original span. These are pooling-head weights, not causal explanations, but they substantiate the claim that the attention head often down-weights greetings/fillers and concentrates on intent-bearing content.

9. **Other clarifications.** We will clean appendix figure/caption/legend inconsistencies and replace "controlled distribution shift" with "mild lexical/style shift." We did not test multi-head/additive/gated pooling; the reported attention head is a single learned token-weighting layer.
